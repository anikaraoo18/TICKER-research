Interpretation 1: Return-seeking policy with a risk-limit overlay
Objective: Maximize the probability of achieving CPI + 4% net of estimated costs over rolling five-year periods, relative to the 60/40 policy benchmark.
Hard constraints:
- Seven-year policy horizon; review quarterly, rebalance annually.
- $750,000 distribution at each quarter-end.
- Maintain at least $3.0 million (12 months’ distributions) in cash or daily-liquid government securities.
- Long-only; no borrowing, short sales, or derivatives.
- No issuer above 8%.
- Annual turnover at or below 25%, absent documented committee approval.
- At least 90% redeemable within five business days in ordinary markets.
- Treat the “no calendar-year loss greater than 10%” statement as a risk target monitored through ex-ante stress limits, rather than an enforceable guarantee.
Invented assumptions:
- “Net” means after implementation, fund, custody, and rebalancing costs.
- Inflation is measured by U.S. CPI.
- The liquidity floor is calculated on total portfolio market value, including the distribution reserve.
- A breach of the 10% calendar-year target is possible; the response is reporting and committee review, not forced selling.
- The benchmark is a comparator, not a tracking-error mandate.
Validation tests:
- Historical and forward-looking five-year rolling return tests against CPI + 4% net.
- Scenario and stress testing for calendar-year losses, including severe equity/bond and inflation shocks.
- Liquidity test: demonstrate at least 90% can be redeemed within five business days while retaining the quarterly-distribution reserve.
- Cash-flow simulation showing all scheduled distributions can be paid without forced asset sales.
- Issuer concentration, leverage, derivatives, turnover, and long-only compliance checks.
- Tracking-error and relative-return analysis versus the 60/40 benchmark.
One CIO question before optimization:
What probability of a calendar-year loss worse than 10% is acceptable, and what action should govern if that threshold is breached or appears likely?
Interpretation 2: Capital-preservation and spending-reliability mandate
Objective: Maximize the probability that all scheduled distributions are funded and that annual losses do not exceed 10%, with CPI + 4% treated as a secondary aspiration rather than the primary optimization target.
Hard constraints:
- All stated legal/operational constraints above.
- The 12-month distribution reserve is a true spending sleeve, segregated from the growth portfolio.
- The remaining portfolio must be managed to a specified annual-loss risk budget intended to support the CIO’s 10% loss limit.
- Annual rebalancing is permitted only when it does not jeopardize liquidity or the loss-risk budget.
Invented assumptions:
- “Cannot have a calendar-year loss greater than 10%” means the committee intends an ex-ante risk-control requirement, such as a maximum modeled probability of crossing −10%.
- The CIO accepts that no long-only, unlevered portfolio can guarantee this result in every market environment without an explicit response mechanism.
- Distribution certainty has priority over maximizing expected return.
- The seven-year horizon does not permit overlooking interim losses because the organization has ongoing spending needs.
- Benchmark underperformance is acceptable when necessary to preserve spending reliability and downside resilience.
Validation tests:
- Monte Carlo or other forward-looking simulation of the probability of meeting all 28 quarterly distributions.
- Annual-loss distribution and stress tests, measuring the probability and severity of losses beyond 10%.
- Sequence-of-returns testing: early adverse-market scenarios combined with scheduled withdrawals.
- Liquidity-under-stress analysis, including redemption timing and the continued availability of four quarters of distributions.
- Five-year CPI + 4% net assessment, explicitly reported alongside the tradeoff against spending reliability and downside control.
- Constraint monitoring for issuer limits, turnover, liquidity, and prohibited exposures.
One CIO question before optimization:
Does “cannot have a calendar-year loss greater than 10%” mean a hard outcome guarantee, a maximum acceptable probability of breach, or a trigger for a specific corrective action?
Both interpretations are defensible, but they optimize different things. The first treats the return objective as primary and the loss statement as a monitored risk target; the second makes spending reliability and downside control primary, likely accepting a lower chance of meeting CPI + 4%.
